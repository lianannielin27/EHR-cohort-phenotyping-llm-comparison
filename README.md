## Purpose of Study:
The purpose of this retrospective cohort study is to evaluate the effectiveness of lisinopril on CKD patients. Lisinopril is a common ACE inhibitor that is used to treat conditions includiong high blood pressure, heart attack recoveries, and diabetic kidney problems. Lisinopril is a common treatment for patients with chronic kindey disease (CKD) to help lower the pressure inside the kidney's filtering units. ACE inhibitors decrease the creation of angiotensin II, which is a vasoconstrictor, thus relaxing blood vessels and allowing easier blood flow. This ultimately reduces the amount of protein (albumin) in the urine, which is otherwise found in CKD patients.

Lisinopril is frequently prescribed after a chronic kidney disease (CKD) diagnosis, especially if the patient also has high blood pressure or protein in their urine (proteinuria). It is taken as an oral tablet once a day. Lisinopril dosage is determined by the specific medical condition being treated, your age, kidney function, and whether you take other medications like diuretics. 

## Methods:
This study uses UCSF De-Identified Research Data to construct a retrospective cohort study as follows:

- Base Cohort: Adults diagnosed with chronic kidney disease who have at least one of the following conditions: albuminuria, hypertension, diabetic nephropathy.
- Lisinopril-Treated Subgroup: Patients from the base cohort who received at least one lisinopril prescription following cohort entry.
- Effectiveness Cohort: Patients who have had baseline creatine/EGFR measurements with sufficient follow-up after prescription.
- Outcome: Creatine levels indicating kidney performance. Creatinine tests are an indicator of kidney function by measuring the muscle waste product in your blood or urine. Healthy kidneys are able to filter this waste out of the blood, so high levels indicate possible kidney malfunction.

### Base Cohort:
- Cohort Entry: Condition era of CKD

## Outcome Extraction: 
To evaluate the efficacy of lisinopril, serum creatinine measurements were compared before and after cohort entry. Baseline values were selected from 365 days before through the index date, and follow-up values were selected from 90–365 days after index. One paired set of measurements was selected per patient, with decreased creatinine indicating improved or preserved kidney function. Changes were evaluated using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with a paired t-test and effect-size calculation as sensitivity analyses.

## Results:
Among 3,762 eligible patients, 3,582 (95.2%) had paired serum or plasma creatinine measurements during the baseline and follow-up periods. Mean creatinine decreased from 1.882 mg/dL (SD 2.141) at baseline to 1.576 mg/dL (SD 1.469) during follow-up. The mean paired change was −0.306 mg/dL (95% CI −0.369 to −0.243). Because the paired differences were markedly skewed and contained extreme values, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used as the primary analysis. Creatinine was significantly lower during follow-up (median change −0.035 mg/dL, IQR −0.246 to 0.123; W=2,437,225.5, p<0.001). The rank-biserial effect size was small (r=−0.184). A paired t-test produced a consistent result, t(3581)=−9.573, p<0.001, with a small paired effect size (dz=−0.160).

Serum creatinine was significantly lower during the 90–365-day follow-up period compared to the baseline. However, the median change and effect sizes were small, indicating that statistical significance can be attributed to the large sample. These findings suggest that lisinopril initiation was associated with a modest improvement or preservation of kidney function. Because this was an uncontrolled retrospective pre–post analysis, we do not have enough evidence to suggest that lisinopril caused the change. More analyses will be needed to have a more definitive conclusion.

## Clinical Note Extraction
Progress notes were selected because they provide longitudinal clinical documentation of CKD management and lisinopril use, including treatment decisions, medication changes, patient response, adverse effects, and clinicians’ assessments. Compared with administrative or procedure-specific notes, progress notes are more likely to capture the ongoing clinical context relevant to evaluating lisinopril treatment.

The 7 days before to 1 year after cohort entry window was chosen to capture both baseline clinical context immediately surrounding lisinopril initiation/entry (−7 days) and short- and longer-term follow-up (+1 year). This allows assessment of treatment decisions and outcomes over time while avoiding unrelated notes from distant periods in the patient’s medical history. To keep inference feasible across SQL, BRIM, and multiple LLMs, 250 eligible patients were randomly sampled and up to two qualifying Progress Notes per patient were retained. Using two notes reduced the risk of under-detecting concepts that may not appear in every note, while limiting patients with extensive documentation from being overrepresented. Patients with only one qualifying note were retained to avoid introducing a requirement for frequent healthcare encounters, which could create selection bias. The final dataset contained 403 notes from 207 patients, including 11 patients with only one qualifying Progress Note.

## Labeling Clinical Notes
### Labeling via Pattern Matching

The SQL pattern-matching analysis can be reproduced in AWS Athena using the provided cohort and clinical-note extraction queries. The workflow can be reproduced as follows:

- Run eligible_progressnotes_sample.sql to start with the eligible CKD cohort and extract Progress Notes from 7 days before through 1 year after cohort entry. This also randomly samples up to 250 eligible patients and retain up to two qualifying Progress Notes per patient. The final analytic sample contained 403 notes from 207 patients.
- Run pattern_matching.sql
- Apply the predefined terms for the five variables: CKD_MENTIONED, ALBUMINURIA_MENTIONED, HYPERTENSION_MENTIONED, DIABETIC_NEPHROPATHY_MENTIONED, and LISINOPRIL_MENTIONED.
- Export the resulting note-level classifications from Athena as CSV/JSON for downstream analysis.

The SQL query uses CASE WHEN statements with case-insensitive text matching to assign a TRUE/FALSE value to each concept for every note. Because the exact search terms and logic are contained in the provided SQL code, users can reproduce the extraction without manually reconstructing the concept definitions.

## Analysis
### Comparison with Milestone 1

Overall, the demographics of patients with note-derived lisinopril mentions were broadly consistent with the structured lisinopril-treated cohort from Milestone 1. In Milestone 1, lisinopril-treated patients had a mean age of 62.31 years and were 59.98% male. Across the four note-extraction approaches, lisinopril-positive patients had mean ages of approximately 59–60 years and were 59–67% male. Race distributions were also generally similar, although some variation was observed because only 20–22 patients were classified as lisinopril-positive by each note-based method.

The proportion of patients with a lisinopril mention in the sampled notes was lower than the proportion with structured lisinopril exposure in Milestone 1. Structured EHR data identified 7,804 of 52,791 eligible patients (14.8%) as lisinopril-treated, whereas the note-based methods identified approximately 9.7–10.6% of the 207-patient note sample. This difference was expected because only up to two Progress Notes per patient were analyzed, and a patient may receive lisinopril without the medication being explicitly mentioned in either sampled note. Therefore, absence of a lisinopril mention should not be interpreted as confirmation that a patient was untreated.

Despite this limitation, lisinopril detection was highly consistent across extraction methods: SQL pattern matching identified 20 patients, BRIM identified 21, GPT-OSS-20B identified 20, and Qwen3-8B-AWQ identified 22. This agreement suggests that the extraction approaches produced relatively stable results for explicit lisinopril mentions, while structured EHR drug-exposure data remains more appropriate for determining actual treatment status.

### Structured EHR Data vs Unstructured Clinical Notes
Overall, the unstructured clinical-note results were directionally consistent with the structured EHR findings but identified a smaller proportion of patients with lisinopril documentation. In Milestone 1, structured drug-exposure data classified 14.8% (7,804/52,791) of eligible CKD patients as lisinopril-treated. In the clinical-note sample, lisinopril was identified in only 9.7–10.6% of patients, although results were highly consistent across SQL, BRIM, GPT-OSS, and Qwen (20–22 of 207 patients).

The demographics were also broadly similar. Structured-EHR lisinopril-treated patients had a mean age of 62.3 years and were 60.0% male, compared with mean ages of approximately 59–60 years and 59–67% male among the note-derived lisinopril-positive groups.

These findings suggest that structured EHR data is more appropriate for identifying treatment exposure, because lisinopril may not be mentioned in the limited Progress Notes sampled for a patient. Conversely, clinical notes provide useful complementary information about the clinical context of treatment, but a medication mention alone does not necessarily indicate active use. Thus, the two data sources were generally consistent demographically, while structured drug-exposure records provide a more direct measure of whether treatment occurred.
